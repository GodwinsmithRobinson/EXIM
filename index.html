1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This DPR outlines the establishment of an export-oriented trading business from India. The business model is to identify international demand, source products from anywhere in India (leveraging the promoter’s base in Karur, Tamil Nadu), and export to buyers across the globe.

India’s Export Performance (FY 2025-26 YTD — Source: DGCIS/TIA Portal): - India’s total exports (Merchandise + Services) for February 2026: ~USD 76.13 Billion (+11.05% YoY) - India trades with 217+ countries, covering 11,700+ commodities (HS8 level) - Top export commodities: Petroleum products, Electronic Goods, Drugs & Pharmaceuticals, Engineering Goods (57% share) - Top destinations: USA, UAE, China, Netherlands, UK (40% share) - India has FTAs with 53 countries (including 27 EU nations under the new India-EU FTA concluded Jan 2026)

Strategic Context — Why Now (Source: Dept. of Commerce / NITI Aayog / DGFT): - Export Promotion Mission (EPM) — NIRYAT PROTSAHAN 2030 Target: Government of India targets USD 2 Trillion in total exports by 2030 (merchandise + services combined) - FY 2025-26 Export Target: Merchandise exports target of USD 500+ Billion; Services exports target of USD 350+ Billion - MSME Contribution: MSMEs contribute approximately 45% of India’s total merchandise exports (~3.3 Cr MSMEs registered on Udyam; ~200,000+ direct MSME exporters — Source: DGFT/Udyam Portal). This share is targeted to grow to 50%+ under EPM. - India’s Global Export Rank: 17th in merchandise exports globally (WTO, 2024); 7th in commercial services exports - FDI in Export-Oriented Sectors: India received USD 70+ Billion FDI in FY 2024-25, with significant flows into export-oriented manufacturing (electronics, pharma, textiles, auto) - PLI (Production Linked Incentive) Scheme Impact: 14 sectors with ₹1.97 lakh crore incentive outlay driving export-capable manufacturing capacity in electronics, pharma, textiles, food processing, auto components, specialty steel - China+1 Strategy: Global buyers actively diversifying supply chains away from China — India is a primary beneficiary, especially in textiles, electronics, pharma, and chemicals

The opportunity is massive. India is the world’s 5th largest economy, and the Government of India actively promotes exports through schemes like RoDTEP, EPCG, Advance Authorization, and the Export Promotion Mission (EPM). The convergence of PLI manufacturing buildup, China+1 diversification, new FTAs (EU, UK, Australia), and digital trade infrastructure (ICEGATE, SWIFT, Niryat Portal) creates the strongest export tailwind India has seen in decades.

1.1 Strategic Positioning for New Entrants

This business is NOT competing with India’s established exporters in hyper-competitive corridors (USA, UAE, Middle East). Instead, the strategy is:

  1. Product Focus: Non-agricultural, non-pharmaceutical, non-perishable products — specifically home textiles, leather goods, brass/copper handicrafts, coir products, and engineered stone. These categories have (a) strong sourcing within Tamil Nadu / South India, (b) India-EU FTA tariff advantages (0-2.5% preferential duty vs 8-12% MFN), and (c) lower regulatory complexity than food/pharma.

  2. Market Focus: Underserved EU and Oceania markets where Indian exporter density is extremely low — Poland, Czech Republic, Romania, New Zealand, Portugal, Sweden, Denmark, Hungary, Ireland, Croatia. These markets have GDP/capita of EUR 15,000-65,000, stable payment cultures, and only 100-500 active Indian exporters (vs 40,000+ in the USA corridor).

  3. Timing Advantage: The India-EU FTA (concluded January 2026) creates a first-mover window of 18-24 months before the market becomes crowded. Early entrants into Eastern European markets will establish buyer relationships, logistics networks, and compliance systems before competitors arrive.

  4. Year 1 Target: EUR 150,000-350,000 in export revenue from 2-3 product categories to 3-5 EU/Oceania markets.

4.2 IEC — Deep Dive (from DGFT Portal)

  • What: Importer-Exporter Code — 10-digit number = PAN of the firm
  • Who needs it: Any entity exporting/importing goods (services export may not need unless claiming FTP benefits)
  • Entity types eligible: Proprietorship, Partnership, LLP, Limited Company, Trust, HUF, Society
  • Prerequisites: PAN, bank account in firm’s name, valid address
  • Fee: ₹500 (online payment)
  • Process: Apply at https://www.dgft.gov.in → Services → IEC → Apply for IEC
  • Documents: Cancelled cheque with entity’s pre-printed name, address proof
  • Annual Update: IEC details must be updated electronically by 30th June every year — even if no changes (confirm unchanged status online)
  • DGFT Helpline: 1800-572-1550 / 1800-111-550

4.3 RCMC — Registration Cum Membership Certificate

Step 14: Quality inspection & testing

Step 20: Customs examination & Let Export Order (LEO)
Step 21: Goods loaded onto vessel/aircraft

Step 23: Submit documents to AD bank for negotiation/collection

Step 25: Buyer receives goods, makes payment
Step 26: Export proceeds received by AD bank → credited to exporter's account

Step 28: Claim export incentives (RoDTEP, Drawback, etc.)

7.4 UNDERSERVED HIGH-POTENTIAL MARKETS — The Smart Beginner Strategy

⚠️ STRATEGIC INSIGHT: India exports to 217+ countries, but ~80% of Indian exporters target the same 8-10 countries (USA, UAE, UK, Germany, Bangladesh, Saudi Arabia, China, Netherlands). These markets are hyper-competitive — a new exporter from Karur competes with 40,000+ established Indian firms, plus Chinese, Vietnamese, Bangladeshi, and Turkish suppliers who have been serving these buyers for decades.

The less-crowded markets — Eastern Europe, Scandinavia, Oceania, Southern Europe, the Baltics, and select Latin American countries — have actual import demand but far fewer Indian suppliers competing. These are YOUR markets if you are a beginner who wants faster buyer acquisition, less price-pressure, higher margins, and better payment culture.

Why Less-Crowded Markets Work for Beginners (Source: DGFT IEC data analysis, FIEO membership patterns, TIA Portal):

  1. Buyers are underserved — they WANT Indian suppliers for textiles, leather, handicrafts but can’t find reliable small-to-medium exporters willing to serve EUR 5,000-20,000 trial orders
  2. Less price-pressure — US/UAE buyers squeeze every cent because they have 50 Indian suppliers quoting; a Polish or Czech buyer with 2-3 Indian options values reliability and compliance over rock-bottom price
  3. India-EU FTA (concluded January 2026) just opened 27 EU markets with preferential tariffs — most established Indian exporters haven’t pivoted yet. First-mover advantage is available NOW for 12-18 months
  4. India-New Zealand FTA (concluded December 2025) — fresh market with near-zero Indian exporter presence beyond dairy/IT
  5. India-EFTA Agreement (March 2024) — Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein — premium markets with minimal Indian competition
  6. Better payment culture — EU/OECD buyers typically honor contracts and pay on time; lower default risk than some African/Middle Eastern/South Asian markets
  7. English widely spoken in business — Poland, Nordics, Netherlands, Baltics all have high English proficiency in B2B commerce
  8. Smaller initial orders — EUR 5,000-20,000 trial orders are manageable for a beginner’s working capital (vs USD 50,000+ from US buyers who expect large minimums)
  9. Higher margins — less competition = less price erosion. Typical margin in EU less-crowded markets: 18-28% vs 10-15% in USA
  10. ECGC Group A countries — all EU/OECD nations are in ECGC’s lowest risk category → lowest premiums, easiest cover

8.2 FTA UTILIZATION — The Hidden Opportunity

Critical Insight: India’s FTA utilization rate is historically only 25-30% — meaning 70-75% of eligible trade does NOT claim the preferential tariff benefit (Source: NITI Aayog / Dept. of Commerce / UNCTAD). Compare this with: - South Korea: ~78% FTA utilization - Japan: ~75% FTA utilization - ASEAN average: ~60% FTA utilization

Why utilization is low: 1. Exporters are unaware the FTA exists for their product/destination 2. Exporters don’t know how to obtain a Preferential Certificate of Origin (PCoO) 3. Rules of Origin (ROO) documentation perceived as complex 4. Small exporters assume it’s not worth the effort

Why this is YOUR competitive advantage: - If your competitor quotes CIF USD 10,000 and the buyer pays 12% MFN duty = landed cost USD 11,200 - You quote CIF USD 10,200 BUT with PCoO under FTA → 0% duty = landed cost USD 10,200 - You win the order despite a higher CIF price because the buyer’s landed cost is lower - This is the #1 pricing strategy veteran exporters use

How to claim FTA benefits: 1. Check if the destination country has an FTA with India (table above) 2. Check if YOUR product’s HS code is covered under the FTA tariff schedule → use ITC Market Access Map (https://www.macmap.org) 3. Verify Rules of Origin — your product must meet the origin criteria (typically: manufactured in India with minimum value addition of 35-40%) 4. Obtain a Preferential Certificate of Origin (PCoO) from the designated agency: - For India-ASEAN FTA: EIC or DGFT-authorized agencies - For India-UAE CEPA: DGFT Trade Connect e-Platform (https://www.trade.gov.in) - For India-EU FTA: FIEO / DGFT Trade Connect - For India-Australia ECTA: FIEO / designated agencies 5. Attach PCoO to shipping documents → buyer’s customs accepts preferential rate

Action: Always check if the destination country has an FTA with India → claim preferential tariff → issue Certificate of Origin from FIEO/relevant EPC. Budget ₹500-2,000 per CoO issuance. The ROI on this small investment is enormous.

Commercial Risks: - Insolvency of the buyer - Buyer’s failure to pay within 4 months of due date (protracted default) - Buyer’s failure to accept goods (subject to conditions)

Political Risks: - Imposition of restrictions on remittances in buyer’s country - War, civil war, revolution in buyer’s country - New import restrictions in buyer’s country - Cancellation of import license - Payment delay due to balance of payment problems

  1. Visit https://main.ecgc.in
  2. Choose appropriate policy
  3. Submit application with export details
  4. ECGC will assess and set credit limits
  5. Pay premium (typically 0.5% to 2% of invoice value, varies by country/buyer risk)
  6. Get policy BEFORE you ship — not after

12.5 Why ECGC is Essential

  • Banks require ECGC cover for granting export finance (packing credit, post-shipment credit)
  • Protects your cash flow against buyer default
  • Enables you to offer credit terms (D/A, D/P) to buyers safely
  • Claims settled by ECGC → AD bank writes off the bill in EDPMS

22.2 Understanding Tariff Structure

24.3 EU MARKET — DETAILED QUALITY & COMPLIANCE REQUIREMENTS

24.3.1 Oeko-Tex Standard 100 — MANDATORY for Textile Exports to EU

Aspect Detail
What is it? World’s leading independent testing and certification system for textiles at all stages of processing — from raw yarns to finished products. Tests for harmful substances.
Why critical? While not legally mandated by EU law, it is a de facto market requirement. 85%+ of EU textile buyers will NOT consider suppliers without Oeko-Tex certification. It is the #1 buyer requirement in home textiles, apparel, and baby products.
What is tested? Banned AZO dyes, formaldehyde, heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury), pesticides, chlorinated phenols, phthalates, organotin compounds, allergenic dyes, pH value, color fastness, VOCs
Product Classes Class I: Baby products (strictest); Class II: Products with direct skin contact; Class III: Products without direct skin contact; Class IV: Decorative materials
How to get certified? Apply through an Oeko-Tex accredited testing institute. In India: Hohenstein India (Coimbatore), SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek. Submit product samples → lab tests → certificate issued within 3-4 weeks.
Cost ₹50,000-1,50,000 per product category per year (depends on product range and number of test reports). Annual renewal required.
Certificate validity 1 year — must be renewed annually with new test reports
Website https://www.oeko-tex.com
Label Physical label with Oeko-Tex logo + certificate number can be attached to every product — this is a powerful selling point
Veteran’s tip Many Karur manufacturers ALREADY have Oeko-Tex certification. Source from Oeko-Tex certified manufacturers and your products are automatically covered. Ask for their certificate copy and lab reports before placing orders.

24.3.2 REACH Regulation (EC 1907/2006) — Chemicals in Products

Aspect Detail
What is REACH? Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals. EU’s regulatory framework for chemical substances.
Why it matters for YOU REACH applies NOT just to chemicals — it applies to any product containing chemical substances. This includes: dyes in textiles, finishes on leather, plasticizers in plastics, coatings on metal products, adhesives in wooden furniture, chrome in leather tanning.
SVHC (Substances of Very High Concern) REACH maintains a “Candidate List” of SVHCs. If your product contains any SVHC above 0.1% by weight, you MUST inform the buyer. Currently 240+ substances on the list.
Key restricted substances for Indian exporters AZO dyes releasing listed amines (textiles/leather); Lead (ceramics, metal products); Cadmium (plastics, jewellery); Chromium VI (leather); Formaldehyde (textiles, wooden products); Nickel (metal items with skin contact); Phthalates (plastics, PVC products)
How to comply Get your products tested at an accredited lab (SGS, Bureau Veritas, TUV, Intertek) for REACH-relevant substances. Obtain a Declaration of Conformity confirming REACH compliance. Cost: ₹15,000-50,000 per product category.
ECHA website https://echa.europa.eu
Penalty for non-compliance EU customs can seize and destroy non-compliant goods. Buyer faces penalties. You lose the buyer permanently.

24.3.3 CE Marking — When Required

Aspect Detail
What is CE? Conformité Européenne — mandatory conformity marking for certain product categories sold in the EU/EEA
Which products need CE? Toys, personal protective equipment (PPE), low voltage electrical equipment, machinery, construction products, medical devices, pressure equipment, gas appliances, recreational craft, measuring instruments
Which products do NOT need CE? Textiles (home textiles, garments) — NO CE required. Furniture (standalone) — generally NO. Handicrafts/decorative items — generally NO. Ceramic tableware — NO (but food-contact regulations apply). Granite/stone — NO (but construction products directive may apply for structural stone).
How to get CE marking Self-declaration by manufacturer (for many product categories) after ensuring compliance with relevant EU Directive + testing. For complex products, notified body assessment required.
Key point for beginners If you’re exporting home textiles, leather goods, handicrafts, or coir products — CE marking is NOT required. Focus on Oeko-Tex (textiles) and REACH (all products) instead.

24.3.4 EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR, 2025+)

Aspect Detail
What? EU’s updated regulation on packaging — requires minimum recycled content, recyclability, and labeling of packaging
Key requirements All packaging entering EU must be recyclable by 2030; minimum recycled content targets for plastic packaging (30% by 2030, 65% by 2040); packaging waste reduction targets

  • ❌ NEVER rely on a single buyer or single country — diversify
  • ❌ NEVER compromise on quality to increase margin — reputation is everything in export
  • ❌ NEVER promise delivery dates you can’t meet — build buffer time

26.3 Operational Traps

  • ❌ NEVER sign a contract you don’t fully understand — always consult a trade lawyer for large orders
  • ❌ NEVER accept buyer’s standard contract without review — check penalty clauses, arbitration clauses
  • ❌ NEVER ignore import duty changes in destination country — tariffs change frequently (especially post-2025 US tariff actions)
  • ❌ NEVER export to sanctioned countries/entities without checking latest DGFT/RBI/UN sanctions list
  • ❌ NEVER mix personal and business bank accounts
  • ❌ NEVER ignore buyer complaints — one bad review can kill future business
  • ❌ NEVER skip due diligence on the buyer — verify their existence, creditworthiness, trade references

34.4 Data Verification Protocol

35.4 Contract Execution Best Practices

  1. ALWAYS have a written contract — even for small orders. An email chain is NOT a substitute
  2. Get the contract reviewed by a trade lawyer before signing — especially for first orders above USD 10,000
  3. Both parties sign two originals — keep one, send one to buyer
  4. Store contracts securely (physical + cloud backup) for at least 8 years (FEMA/Income Tax requirement)
  5. Proforma Invoice is NOT a contract — it’s a quote. The contract comes AFTER the PI is accepted
  6. For repeat orders, use a Master Supply Agreement (MSA) — individual POs reference the MSA terms

37. COMMON EXPORT FRAUDS & HOW TO AVOID THEM

37.1 Critical Warning

Export fraud is REAL and costs Indian exporters crores every year. A 20-year veteran has seen every type of fraud. Here are the most common ones:

37.2 Types of Export Fraud

# Fraud Type How It Works Red Flags Prevention
1 Advance Fee Fraud Buyer says they’ll place a huge order but need you to pay “fees” upfront (inspection fee, registration fee, customs clearance fee in their country) Request for upfront payment FROM buyer, unusually large order from unknown buyer, urgency NEVER pay any fee to a buyer. Legitimate buyers don’t ask sellers to pay fees.
2 Buyer Impersonation Fraudster impersonates a known company (uses similar email domain, fake website) to place an order, gets goods shipped to a different address Email domain slightly different (e.g., @walmart-int.com vs @walmart.com), different shipping address than company address Verify buyer’s email domain, call their official number, check company registry
3 L/C Fraud Fake/forged Letter of Credit from a non-existent or shell bank L/C from unknown bank, bank not on SWIFT network, L/C terms too good to be true Verify L/C through YOUR AD bank. Bank will authenticate via SWIFT. Never ship against unconfirmed L/C.
4 Payment Diversion After building trust with small orders (paid), buyer places large order on D/A terms and disappears after getting goods Sudden jump in order size, request to change payment terms, buyer moving address Increase order size gradually. Never change payment terms suddenly. ECGC cover mandatory.
5 Shipping Agent Fraud Fraudulent freight forwarder issues fake Bill of Lading, goods never actually shipped (or diverted) Unknown forwarder, unusually low freight rates, forwarder not IATA/FIATA registered Use only reputed, licensed freight forwarders. Verify IATA/FIATA membership.
6 Quality Dispute Fraud Buyer receives goods, claims quality issues (without evidence), demands discount or refuses payment No pre-shipment inspection agreed, buyer rejects without third-party assessment Always do pre-shipment inspection by SGS/Bureau Veritas/Intertek. Photograph everything.
7 Sample Fraud Buyer requests expensive samples repeatedly but never places an order — using your samples to source cheaper from elsewhere Repeated sample requests without order, requests for large sample quantities, no commitment timeline Charge for samples (deduct from first order). Limit sample quantities.
8 Document Fraud Buyer presents forged documents (fake import permit, fake CoO) or exporter creates fraudulent documents Unusual document formats, unverifiable stamps/signatures Verify all documents from issuing authority. Cross-check with embassy.
9 Nigerian Prince / West African Advance Fee Elaborate story about a large government tender in African country, requiring agent fees/commissions upfront Government contracts from unknown officials, request for cash payments, elaborate backstory Never pay fees upfront. Verify through Indian Embassy in that country.
10 Phishing / Cyber Fraud Fraudster hacks buyer’s email, sends exporter modified bank details for payment — payment goes to fraudster’s account Sudden change in bank details, email language slightly different, request to wire to different country ALWAYS call buyer directly (on known number) to verify bank details before wiring. Use secure email.

37.3 Veteran’s Anti-Fraud Checklist

  1. ✅ Verify buyer’s legal existence — Check company registry of their country (e.g., Companies House UK, SEC EDGAR US)
  2. ✅ Get a D&B (Dun & Bradstreet) report or ECGC buyer assessment BEFORE shipping on credit
  3. ✅ Google the company + “fraud” or “scam” — yes, a simple search helps
  4. ✅ Verify email domain — check WHOIS (https://whois.domaintools.com) to see if the domain is new or suspicious
  5. ✅ Never ship to a third-party address different from the buyer’s registered address without verified explanation
  6. ✅ Use ECGC — even the premium is worth it compared to one default
  7. ✅ Photograph EVERYTHING — goods, packaging, loading, sealing of container — with date stamps
  8. ✅ Insist on L/C for new buyers — if they refuse even after initial orders, that’s a red flag
  9. ✅ Trust your instinct — if something feels too good to be true, it probably is
  10. ✅ Report fraud — to local police, ECGC, and DGFT. Also alert other exporters through FIEO.

38. POST-EXPORT COMPLIANCE (EDPMS, eBRC, RECONCILIATION)

38.1 Why Post-Export Compliance Matters

Shipping goods is only HALF the job. A veteran exporter knows that post-export compliance is where most exporters get penalized. RBI and AD banks monitor every export transaction through EDPMS.

38.2 EDPMS — Export Data Processing and Monitoring System

Aspect Detail
What is EDPMS? RBI’s system to track all export transactions end-to-end — from shipping bill to payment realization
Who reports? AD banks report to RBI; data flows from Customs (shipping bill) → AD bank → RBI
What is tracked? Shipping bill number, export value, buyer details, payment received, date of realization
Why it matters? Every shipping bill MUST be “closed” in EDPMS — payment realized, bill matched
Open bills in EDPMS = trouble RBI issues caution list for exporters with large open/overdue bills
Managed by Reserve Bank of India

38.3 Post-Export Compliance Timeline

DAY 0:       Goods exported → Shipping Bill generated on ICEGATE
DAY 1-21:    Submit shipping documents to AD bank (FEMA deadline: 21 days)
DAY 1-270:   Export proceeds MUST be realized (9 months = 270 days from export date)
              → AD bank credits forex to your account
              → AD bank reports realization in EDPMS
              → Shipping bill status changes to "Realized" in EDPMS
WITHIN 15 DAYS of realization:
              → AD bank generates eBRC (Electronic Bank Realization Certificate)
AFTER eBRC:  → Claim export incentives (RoDTEP, Drawback) — eBRC needed as proof
              → File GST returns (if IGST refund applicable)
              → Shipping bill "closed" in EDPMS

38.4 eBRC — Electronic Bank Realization Certificate

Aspect Detail
What? Digital proof that export proceeds have been realized (received in India)
Issued by? AD bank — auto-generated when export proceeds are received and matched
Where to check? DGFT portal → Services → eBRC → View eBRC status
Why needed? Required to claim RoDTEP, Duty Drawback, EPCG obligation fulfillment, Star Export House status
Problem if missing? Cannot claim incentives; IEC may be flagged in DGFT system

38.5 Write-Off Rules (RBI)

If the buyer doesn’t pay and you can’t recover:

Category Write-Off Limit Authority
Self-write-off (normal exporters) Up to 5% of previous year’s total export realization AD bank can approve
Self-write-off (status holders — Star Export Houses) Up to 10% of previous year’s total export realization AD bank can approve
Beyond self-write-off limit Case-by-case RBI approval required through AD bank

Conditions for write-off: - Exporter must have exhausted all efforts to recover payment - ECGC claim must have been filed and settled (if covered) - AD bank is satisfied that the non-realization is not due to exporter’s fault - Proper documentation of recovery efforts required

38.6 Caution List / Defaulters List

  • RBI maintains a caution list of exporters with significantly overdue export bills
  • Being on this list = difficulty getting export finance, ECGC may refuse cover
  • Prevention: Monitor your EDPMS status regularly through your AD bank; don’t let bills stay open beyond 9 months

39. TAX IMPLICATIONS FOR EXPORT BUSINESS

39.1 Income Tax on Export Income

Aspect Detail
Tax Rate Export income is taxed as normal business income — 25% for companies (turnover up to ₹400 Cr) or 30% (above ₹400 Cr) + surcharge + cess
Section 10AA — SEZ Deduction Units in SEZ: 100% exemption on export profit for first 5 years, 50% for next 5 years, 50% of ploughed-back profit for next 5 years (being phased out for new units post-2020)

39.3 DTAA (Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements)

  • India has DTAAs with 95+ countries (Source: Income Tax Department)
  • Purpose: Avoid being taxed on the same income in both India and the buyer’s country
  • Most relevant for: Commission income, royalty, fees for technical services, business profits
  • Action: Before receiving commission/royalty from a foreign party, check if DTAA exists with that country
  • Check DTAAs: https://www.incometax.gov.in → International Taxation → DTAA

39.4 Transfer Pricing (If Applicable)

  • Applies to international transactions with “Associated Enterprises” (related parties)
  • If you set up a subsidiary abroad or export to a related entity, the transfer price must be at “arm’s length”
  • Maintaining Transfer Pricing documentation is mandatory
  • Penalty for non-compliance: 2% of transaction value
  • Governed by Sections 92 to 92F of Income Tax Act
  • Veteran’s advice: If doing related-party international transactions, hire a CA specializing in transfer pricing

39.5 Custom Duty on Import Side

When importing inputs for re-export or importing finished goods for trading:

Duty Component Description
Basic Customs Duty (BCD) Standard duty based on HS code — ranges from 0% to 150%
Social Welfare Surcharge (SWS) 10% of BCD
IGST Applied on (CIF value + BCD + SWS) — rate based on HS code (5%/12%/18%/28%)
Compensation Cess On certain goods (luxury, sin goods) — additional to IGST
Anti-Dumping Duty (ADD) If applicable — on specific products from specific countries
Safeguard Duty If applicable — temporary protection duty

40. IMPORT BUSINESS PROCESS (A TO Z)

40.1 Why Include Import?

This DPR covers import-export. While the primary focus is export, understanding the import side is essential because: 1. You may need to import raw materials/inputs for export production 2. You may find import trading opportunities (buying abroad, selling in India) 3. Advance Authorization and EPCG schemes involve duty-free imports against export obligations 4. Many successful EXIM houses do both — it diversifies revenue

40.2 Step-by-Step Import Process

Step 1:  Ensure IEC is active (same IEC used for import and export)
Step 2:  Check ITC-HS Import Policy for the product at https://www.dgft.gov.in
         → Product may be: FREE / RESTRICTED / PROHIBITED / STE (State Trading Enterprise)
Step 3:  If RESTRICTED → apply for import license from DGFT
Step 4:  If FREE → no license needed (but may need BIS/FSSAI/other compliance)
Step 5:  Negotiate with foreign supplier — agree on price, Incoterm, payment terms
Step 6:  Open L/C through AD bank (if L/C terms) OR arrange advance payment (within FEMA limits)
Step 7:  Supplier ships goods → receive shipping documents (B/L, invoice, packing list, CoO, etc.)
Step 8:  File Bill of Entry on ICEGATE → engage Customs Broker
Step 9:  Pay customs duty (BCD + SWS + IGST + any ADD/safeguard duty)
         → Assessed by Customs → auto-assessment or officer assessment
Step 10: Customs examination (if selected under RMS)
Step 11: Out of Charge (OOC) order → goods released from customs
Step 12: Take delivery from port → transport to warehouse
Step 13: Record in books → claim IGST as Input Tax Credit (ITC) in GST return
Step 14: If importing under Advance Authorization → no duty paid; fulfill export obligation later

40.5 Advance Authorization for Import (Duty-Free Imports)

  • If you import inputs to manufacture goods for export → apply for Advance Authorization from DGFT
  • Inputs imported at ZERO customs duty (BCD + SWS + IGST waived)
  • Must fulfill export obligation (typically 6x duty saved, within 18 months extendable to 36 months)
  • Apply at: https://www.dgft.gov.in → Services → Advance Authorisation
  • Based on SION (Standard Input Output Norms) published by DGFT — specifies how much input needed per unit of output

40.6 Key Import Compliance Points

  • Check import policy for your HS code at DGFT portal BEFORE ordering
  • Certain goods require pre-import condition under Advance Authorization
  • Anti-Dumping Duties may apply on specific products from specific countries (check CBIC notifications)
  • Import payment must be made within 6 months of import (FEMA rule)
  • Bill of Entry must be filed within 30 days of vessel arrival (else demurrage charges)
  • Maintain proper import documentation for 8 years (Income Tax/Customs)

41.3 SCOMET — India’s Strategic Trade Controls

  • India regulates export of dual-use items (items that can be used for both civilian and military purposes)
  • SCOMET List published by DGFT — categorized into:
  • Category 0: Nuclear materials, equipment, technology
  • Category 1: Toxic chemicals and precursors (Chemical Weapons Convention)
  • Category 2: Microorganisms, toxins (Biological Weapons Convention)
  • Category 3: Materials, materials processing equipment
  • Category 4: Nuclear-related equipment
  • Category 5: Aerospace systems, propulsion
  • Category 6: Munitions
  • Category 7: Electronics, computers
  • Category 8: Marine and aerospace special technologies
  • Handbook: https://content.dgft.gov.in/Website/NCSTC+2026+Handbook+on+India’s+Strategic+Trade+Control+System+(13.01.2026).pdf
  • If your product falls under SCOMET: Must obtain export license from DGFT before export
  • Penalty for violation: Prosecution under FT(D&R) Act, imprisonment, heavy fines

41.4 Sanctions Screening Process (DO THIS FOR EVERY TRANSACTION)

Step 1: Check buyer's name + address against UN sanctions list
Step 2: Check against DGFT denied entity list
Step 3: If payment in USD → check OFAC SDN list
Step 4: If buyer in EU → check EU consolidated sanctions
Step 5: Check product against SCOMET list (HS code mapping available)
Step 6: Check ECGC country classification for buyer's country
Step 7: If ANY match → STOP. Consult DGFT / trade lawyer before proceeding
Step 8: Document your screening (date, lists checked, result) — keep for 8 years

42.3 Freight Negotiation Tips (from 20 years of experience)

  1. Get quotes from at least 3 freight forwarders — rates vary significantly
  2. Book early — last-minute bookings cost 20-50% more
  3. Negotiate long-term contracts with shipping lines if you’re shipping regularly (quarterly/annual contracts)
  4. Understand surcharges — base freight is just one component: - BAF (Bunker Adjustment Factor) — fuel surcharge - CAF (Currency Adjustment Factor) - THC (Terminal Handling Charges) — origin and destination - ISPS (International Ship and Port Facility Security) - Documentation fees - EBS (Emergency Bunker Surcharge) - GRI (General Rate Increase) — periodic increases
  5. Compare FCL vs LCL: - FCL is cheaper per CBM if you can fill a container - LCL works for small volumes but adds consolidation/deconsolidation charges - Break-even: Typically, if your cargo exceeds 12-15 CBM, FCL is more economical
  6. Free time matters: Negotiate free days at destination (detention-free days) — prevents buyer’s demurrage charges
  7. Direct vs Transshipment: Direct sailings are faster and less risky; transshipment adds 5-10 days and risk of delays/mishandling

42.4 Demurrage & Detention — The Silent Profit Killers

Term Definition Who Pays
Demurrage Charges for keeping a container at the port/terminal beyond free time Typically consignee (buyer), but can become your problem if you’re DDP
Detention Charges for keeping a container outside the port/terminal beyond free time Consignee (buyer)
Per Diem Charges Daily rate for demurrage/detention Varies: USD 50-150/day for dry containers, USD 100-300/day for reefers

Veteran’s tip: Inform the buyer about vessel arrival date well in advance. Most disputes with buyers start with unexpected demurrage charges.

44. PACKAGING STANDARDS & LABELING REQUIREMENTS

44.1 ISPM-15 — Wood Packaging Material Standards

  • ISPM-15 = International Standards for Phytosanitary Measures No. 15
  • Applies to ALL wood packaging material used in international trade (pallets, crates, dunnage)
  • Wood must be heat treated (HT) or methyl bromide treated (MB) and marked with the ISPM-15 stamp
  • If not compliant: Consignment can be rejected, quarantined, or destroyed at destination port
  • Indian accredited agencies provide ISPM-15 treatment and certification
  • Alternative: Use non-wood packaging (plastic pallets, plywood, MDF) — ISPM-15 does not apply

46.1 What Are Buying Agents?

In the real world of export, many buyers don’t source directly from Indian exporters. They work through buying agents (also called indenting agents, commission agents, or sourcing agents) who: - Operate in India on behalf of foreign buyers - Source products, conduct quality inspections, coordinate logistics - Earn commission (typically 3-7%) from the buyer or the exporter

46.2 Types of Agents in Export Business

Agent Type Role Commission Who Pays

46.3 Key Considerations When Working with Agents

  1. FEMA compliance: Commission payments to foreign agents must be declared and routed through AD bank
  2. Commission limit (RBI): Generally up to 12.5% of invoice value for trade commission (check current RBI master direction)
  3. TDS: If paying commission to Indian agent — TDS under Section 194H (5% for residents)
  4. If paying commission to foreign agent — check DTAA for applicable TDS rate; withholding tax obligations under Section 195
  5. Written agency agreement — ALWAYS have a written contract specifying commission rate, territory, exclusivity (if any), termination, non-compete
  6. Verify the agent — check references, past clients, market reputation
  7. Avoid agents who demand upfront payment — red flag for fraud

46.4 Finding Reliable Agents

  • Indian Embassies maintain lists of buying agents in their country
  • FIEO can recommend vetted agents
  • Trade fairs are the best place to meet genuine agents
  • Industry-specific EPCs have agent networks

50.1 Why Supplier Management is Critical

50.2 Approved Vendor List (AVL) Framework

Step Action
1 Identify potential suppliers — trade directories, industry associations, local cluster networks (Tirupur for knitwear, Karur for home textiles, etc.)

50.3 Supplier Agreement Key Points

  • Quality specifications attached as annexure (measurable parameters)
  • Delivery timeline with penalties for delay
  • Payment terms — negotiate 30-60 day credit from suppliers (standard in India)
  • Rejection and replacement clause — who bears cost of rejected goods
  • Exclusivity — consider for key products (prevents supplier from selling same to your competitors)

50.4 Backup Supplier Strategy

  • Split orders — 60-40 or 70-30 between primary and backup supplier
  • Develop backup in a different geography — e.g., if primary is in Karur, backup in Erode or Salem
  • Never let one supplier become irreplaceable — they will eventually exploit it

51. WORKING CAPITAL CYCLE MANAGEMENT

51.1 The Cash-to-Cash Cycle in Export

The working capital cycle in export is long — this is the #1 reason new exporters struggle:

EXPORT WORKING CAPITAL CYCLE
================================

Day 0:    Receive confirmed order / L/C from buyer
Day 1-7:  Apply for packing credit from AD bank (bank credits 80-90% of order value)
Day 7-30: Procure goods from Indian supplier (pay supplier within 30-60 days)
Day 30-45: Quality check, packaging, documentation, customs clearance, loading
Day 45-50: Goods shipped → Bill of Lading issued
Day 50-55: Submit documents to AD bank → bank sends to buyer's bank
Day 55-90: Transit time (sea freight to USA: 25-35 days)
Day 90-120: Buyer receives goods, inspects, accepts
Day 100-140: Payment received by AD bank
Day 140-150: Bank credits your account, deducts packing credit + interest

TOTAL CYCLE: 90-150 DAYS (sometimes longer)

51.2 How to Manage Working Capital

Strategy How It Helps
Packing Credit from AD bank Bank finances 80-90% of order value at concessional interest — you don’t need to invest full amount
Advance Payment from buyer Shortens cycle — you have cash before procurement
Supplier credit (30-60 days) You pay supplier after getting payment from buyer
Export Bill Discounting Bank pays you immediately against export bill — you don’t wait for buyer’s payment
ECGC policy Banks give better terms (higher limits, lower interest) with ECGC cover
Multiple orders staggered Cash flow from earlier orders funds later ones — steady pipeline
Interest Equalization Scheme Government subvention of 2-5% on export credit interest — reduces your cost

51.3 Working Capital Calculation Example

EXAMPLE: First Export Order — Home Textiles to USA

Order Value:                      USD 25,000 (₹21,00,000 @ ₹84/USD)
Packing Credit from Bank (85%):   ₹17,85,000
Your Investment (15%):             ₹3,15,000

Supplier Cost (70% of FOB):       ₹14,70,000
Logistics + Docs + Insurance:     ₹1,50,000
Bank Charges + Interest:           ₹50,000
ECGC Premium:                      ₹15,000
Miscellaneous:                     ₹30,000
TOTAL COST:                        ₹17,15,000

Margin: ₹21,00,000 - ₹17,15,000 = ₹3,85,000 (~18.3%)
Actual Cash Invested: ₹3,15,000
Return on Cash Invested: ₹3,85,000 / ₹3,15,000 = 122% (on your invested capital)

But... this cash is locked for 90-150 days.

58. INCOTERMS 2020 — PRACTICAL DEEP DIVE

58.1 What Are Incoterms?

Incoterms® (International Commercial Terms) are published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC). They define the responsibilities, costs, and risks between buyer and seller in international trade.

Current version: Incoterms® 2020 (effective January 1, 2020)

58.2 All 11 Incoterms 2020 — Practical Implications

FOR ANY MODE OF TRANSPORT (7 terms):

Incoterm Exporter’s Cost Responsibility Risk Transfer Point Insurance Obligation Practical Use
EXW (Ex Works) None — goods ready at your premises At your premises None Rarely used in export — buyer handles everything. Customs clearance is YOUR problem if buyer can’t do it in India.
FCA (Free Carrier) Deliver to carrier/named place + export customs When goods delivered to carrier None Best for air/multimodal. Tip: FCA with “On Board” notation replaces FOB for containers.
CPT (Carriage Paid To) Freight to destination When goods delivered to first carrier (at origin!) None Exporter pays freight but risk transfers at origin — buyer’s risk during transit!
CIP (Carriage & Insurance Paid To) Freight + Insurance to destination When goods delivered to first carrier YES — minimum ICC-A (all risk) Like CPT but with insurance. Incoterms 2020 upgraded CIP to ICC-A (was ICC-C).
DAP (Delivered At Place) All costs to destination (excl. import duty) At destination, before unloading Recommended but not mandatory Door delivery minus import duties. Good for markets where buyer can’t handle logistics.
DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded) All costs + unloading at destination After unloading at destination Recommended but not mandatory New in 2020 (replaced DAT). Exporter bears unloading risk. Rare.
DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) ALL costs including import duty, customs, delivery At buyer’s premises Recommended but not mandatory Maximum responsibility on exporter. Avoid unless you know destination country’s import procedure well.

FOR SEA & INLAND WATERWAY ONLY (4 terms):

Incoterm Exporter’s Cost Responsibility Risk Transfer Point Insurance Obligation Practical Use
FAS (Free Alongside Ship) Deliver goods alongside vessel at port Alongside vessel None Rare — mainly for bulk cargo like coal, grain
FOB (Free On Board) Deliver goods on board vessel + export customs On board vessel at origin port None MOST COMMON for sea export. Buyer arranges freight & insurance. Price: “FOB Chennai Port”
CFR (Cost and Freight) Freight to destination port On board vessel at origin port (!) None Exporter pays freight but risk transfers when goods loaded at origin. Buyer at risk during sea transit!
CIF (Cost, Insurance & Freight) Freight + Insurance to destination port On board vessel at origin port (!) YES — minimum ICC-C (basic) Very common. But note: risk transfers at origin, not destination! Insurance minimum is only ICC-C under Incoterms 2020.

58.3 Critical Veteran Insights on Incoterms

Insight Detail
FOB ≠ Container-safe ICC recommends FCA for containerized cargo, not FOB. FOB risk transfers “on board” but containers are handed to carrier before loading. However, most Indian exporters still use FOB — it’s industry practice.
CIF insurance is MINIMUM only CIF only requires ICC-C (basic cover). If buyer wants ICC-A (all risk), negotiate and price accordingly. Under CIP, ICC-A is now mandatory (Incoterms 2020 change).
Risk ≠ Cost In CFR and CIF, exporter pays freight but risk transfers at origin. This means if goods are damaged in transit, it’s the BUYER’s loss (even though exporter paid the freight). This confuses many people.
DDP is risky for exporter You’re responsible for everything including import duty in a foreign country. If you don’t know that country’s customs system, avoid DDP.
Always specify version Write “CIF Mumbai, Incoterms® 2020” — not just “CIF”. Different versions have different rules.
Incoterms don’t transfer ownership Incoterms define cost and risk — NOT ownership/title. Title transfer is governed by the sales contract.
Incoterms don’t define payment terms Payment terms (L/C, T/T, D/P) are separate from Incoterms. Specify both in the contract.

58.4 Which Incoterm to Choose?

Situation Recommended Incoterm Why
New exporter, sea shipment FOB You control export side only; buyer handles freight/insurance in their market
Buyer wants all-inclusive price (sea) CIF You include freight + insurance in price — easier for buyer to compare
Air/multimodal shipment FCA or CIP Designed for non-sea transport
Door delivery, you know the market DAP For markets where you have experience with local logistics
Buyer insists on DDP DDP — but price it carefully Include all possible import duties, customs charges, local taxes in your quote
High-value goods, want to control quality CIF or CIP You arrange insurance, ensuring proper coverage

59. FOREX RISK MANAGEMENT & HEDGING STRATEGIES

59.1 Why Forex Risk is Critical

When you quote USD 17,552 FOB today at ₹84/USD, you expect to receive ₹14,74,410. But if the INR strengthens to ₹82/USD by the time payment arrives (90-150 days later), you receive only ₹14,39,264 — a loss of ₹35,146 on a single order. On annual turnover of USD 500,000, a 2-rupee movement means ₹10,00,000 swing. Veterans don’t gamble on exchange rates.

59.2 Types of Forex Risk in Export

Risk Type Description When It Hits
Transaction Risk Exchange rate changes between quotation date and payment receipt date Every order — the primary risk
Translation Risk Value of foreign currency receivables on your balance sheet date Annual accounts / audit
Economic Risk Long-term currency trend makes your products uncompetitive Strategic — if INR weakens, exports become more competitive; if INR strengthens, exports suffer

59.3 Hedging Instruments Available to Indian Exporters (Source: RBI / AD Banks)

Instrument How It Works Cost Best For Minimum Size
Forward Contract Lock in exchange rate today for future date. Bank guarantees the rate. 0.1-0.5% of contract value (premium/discount based on interest rate differential) Orders with known payment date, >USD 10,000 Usually USD 5,000-10,000 minimum
Option Contract Right (not obligation) to sell USD at a specified rate (strike price) on/before expiry Option premium: 0.5-2% of contract value Uncertain payment date, want to benefit from favorable moves USD 25,000+ (varies by bank)
Forward with Cancellation Forward contract that can be cancelled (with cancellation charges) Slightly higher than plain forward When order may be cancelled/delayed Same as forward
EEFC Account Keep forex in Exchange Earners Foreign Currency account — convert when rate is favorable No cost (but non-interest bearing, must convert by end of next month — RBI rule) Short-term tactical; small amounts No minimum
Natural Hedge If you import inputs denominated in USD and export in USD — currency movements cancel out Free! Import-export businesses (EXIM houses) N/A

59.4 Practical Hedging Strategy for New Exporters

RECOMMENDED HEDGING APPROACH
=============================

1. ORDERS < USD 10,000:
   → Don't hedge — spread across multiple small orders averages out.
   → Use EEFC account to time conversion.

2. ORDERS USD 10,000 - 50,000:
   → Book a FORWARD CONTRACT for 50-70% of the expected receipt amount.
   → Leave 30-50% unhedged to benefit from favorable movements.
   → Book forward on the day you confirm the order / receive L/C.

3. ORDERS > USD 50,000:
   → Book FORWARD CONTRACT for 70-80% of expected receipt.
   → Consider OPTION CONTRACT for remaining 20-30% if payment date uncertain.
   → Consult your AD bank's forex desk — they will advise based on market outlook.

4. ANNUAL POLICY (when doing regular exports):
   → Hedge 60-70% of projected annual forex receipts using forward contracts.
   → Review and roll over quarterly.
   → Never be 100% hedged — you want some exposure to favorable moves.

59.5 Forward Contract — Worked Example

FORWARD CONTRACT EXAMPLE
=========================
Order: USD 25,000, FOB Chennai
Expected payment: 90 days from today
Spot rate today: ₹84.20/USD
90-day forward rate offered by bank: ₹83.80/USD (forward discount because
  Indian interest rates > US interest rates)

You BOOK the forward: Sell USD 25,000 at ₹83.80 after 90 days
Guaranteed receipt: 25,000 × 83.80 = ₹20,95,000

SCENARIO A — INR strengthens to ₹82.00:
  Without forward: 25,000 × 82.00 = ₹20,50,000 → LOSS of ₹45,000
  With forward: ₹20,95,000 → SAVED ₹45,000 ✓

SCENARIO B — INR weakens to ₹86.00:
  Without forward: 25,000 × 86.00 = ₹21,50,000 → GAIN of ₹55,000
  With forward: ₹20,95,000 → MISSED ₹55,000 (but you locked in a known rate)

DECISION: Hedging is INSURANCE, not speculation. A veteran always hedges
large orders because one adverse move can wipe out the entire margin.

59.6 Key RBI Rules on Forex Hedging (Source: RBI Master Direction on Risk Management)

  • Exporters can book forward contracts for export receivables based on confirmed orders, past performance, or contracted exports
  • Forward contracts can be cancelled and rebooked — but cancellation charges apply
  • AD banks may allow forward booking up to average of previous 3 years’ exports even without specific order
  • Options are available on recognized exchanges (NSE, BSE) and through AD banks (OTC)
  • EEFC Account: 100% of forex can be retained temporarily; must be converted by end of next month (unless used for permitted payments)

60. L/C DOCUMENT FLOW — STEP-BY-STEP BANK PROCESS

60.1 Why Understanding L/C Flow is Critical

A Letter of Credit (L/C) is the most secure payment method for export (other than advance payment). But document discrepancies under L/C are the #1 cause of payment delays and disputes. A veteran exporter knows the L/C document flow like the back of their hand.

60.2 L/C Life Cycle — Complete Flow

L/C DOCUMENT FLOW — END TO END
================================

PHASE 1: L/C ISSUANCE
───────────────────────
[1] Exporter and Buyer agree on terms (PI accepted, PO received)
     ↓
[2] Buyer instructs THEIR bank (Issuing Bank) to open L/C in your favor
     ↓
[3] Issuing Bank issues L/C via SWIFT (MT700 message) to YOUR bank (Advising Bank / AD Bank)
     ↓
[4] YOUR AD Bank receives L/C, verifies authenticity (SWIFT authentication)
     ↓
[5] AD Bank advises (forwards) the L/C to you
     ↓
[6] YOU review L/C terms — check EVERY clause against your PI/PO:
    ✓ Beneficiary name, address, IEC
    ✓ Product description, HS code, quantity
    ✓ Price, currency, Incoterm
    ✓ Shipment date (latest date of shipment)
    ✓ Presentation period (typically 21 days from B/L date)
    ✓ Expiry date and place
    ✓ Documents required (exactly which documents, how many copies)
    ✓ Port of loading, port of discharge
    ✓ Partial shipments allowed? Transshipment allowed?
    ✓ Any special conditions
     ↓
[7] If ANY term doesn't match → request AMENDMENT from buyer BEFORE shipment
    Buyer instructs issuing bank → amendment sent via SWIFT → your bank advises

PHASE 2: SHIPMENT & DOCUMENT PREPARATION
──────────────────────────────────────────
[8] Source goods, quality check, pack, arrange logistics (as per L/C terms)
     ↓
[9] Ship goods → obtain Bill of Lading (B/L) or Airway Bill (AWB)
     ↓
[10] Prepare ALL documents EXACTLY as specified in the L/C:
     - Commercial Invoice (number of copies as per L/C)
     - Packing List
     - Bill of Lading (full set 3/3 originals typically)
     - Certificate of Origin (type as per L/C — preferential/non-preferential)
     - Insurance Certificate (if CIF/CIP — coverage as per L/C)
     - Inspection Certificate (if required in L/C)
     - Any other document mentioned in L/C

PHASE 3: DOCUMENT PRESENTATION & NEGOTIATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────
[11] Submit ALL original documents to YOUR AD Bank
     — within the presentation period specified in L/C (typically 21 days from B/L date)
     — AND before L/C expiry date
     ↓
[12] AD Bank examines documents (takes 5 banking days as per UCP 600)
     → If COMPLIANT (no discrepancies): Bank NEGOTIATES the documents
        = Bank pays you (or credits your account) against the export bill
     → If DISCREPANT: Bank informs you — options:
        a) Correct the documents (if correctable — e.g., minor typo)
        b) Request buyer's waiver of discrepancies (buyer must accept)
        c) Send documents on "approval basis" (risky — buyer can refuse)
     ↓
[13] AD Bank sends documents to Issuing Bank via courier (DHL/FedEx)
     — along with covering schedule (list of documents sent)
     — demand for payment (sight L/C) or acceptance (usance L/C)

PHASE 4: PAYMENT
─────────────────
[14] Issuing Bank receives documents, examines (5 banking days per UCP 600)
     → If COMPLIANT: Issuing Bank debits buyer's account → remits payment via SWIFT to your AD Bank
     → If DISCREPANT: Issuing Bank contacts buyer for waiver or refuses
     ↓
[15] YOUR AD Bank receives payment (SWIFT MT103 credit)
     ↓
[16] AD Bank credits your current account (after deducting bank charges + any packing credit repayment)
     ↓
[17] AD Bank reports realization in EDPMS → shipping bill closed
     ↓
[18] AD Bank generates eBRC → you claim RoDTEP / Drawback incentives

TIMELINE (Typical for L/C at Sight — Sea Shipment):
  L/C received → Shipment: 15-30 days
  Document presentation: Within 21 days of B/L
  Bank examination: 5 days
  Document transit (India → buyer's country): 5-7 days
  Issuing bank examination: 5 days
  Payment remittance: 2-3 days via SWIFT
  TOTAL: 30-60 days from shipment to payment receipt

61.5 Lessons from This Case Study

  1. Total time from order to payment: ~60 days
  2. Cash invested by exporter: Only ~₹2.14 lakh (rest financed by bank packing credit + buyer advance)
  3. Profit of ₹5.1 lakh on a single ₹14.3 lakh order — this is the power of export margins + incentives
  4. Export incentives (RoDTEP + Drawback) added ₹88,500 to the bottom line — never skip claiming them
  5. ECGC + marine insurance cost was ₹7,300 — trivial compared to the protection they provide
  6. Repeat order potential: If buyer is satisfied, expect repeat order every 60-90 days → annual revenue: USD 70,000-100,000 from THIS SINGLE BUYER
